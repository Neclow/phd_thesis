\section{Pandemic preparedness}

In a similar vein to other emergencies, pandemic management can be systematically categorised into four distinct stages: prevention (or mitigation), preparedness, response, and recovery~\cite{alexander2002, coppola2006}. \emph{Pandemic prevention} and \textit{pandemic preparedness} are often defined conjointly as a set of measures to protect human populations through concerted national and international efforts~\cite{paho2024}. According to the \gls{who}, key measures for pandemic prevention and preparedness are clustered under five categories: emergency coordination, collaborative surveillance, community protection, clinical care, and access to countermeasures~\cite{who2023, paho2024, who2025}. Recently, the One Health approach~\cite{onehealth2008} has become a cornerstone of pandemic preparedness frameworks, such as the \gls{who} Pandemic Agreement~\cite{who2025}. This holistic view emphasises the importance of monitoring ecological and environmental factors, thereby extending surveillance beyond human populations.

The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the benefits of collecting large-scale genomic datasets for getting a more holistic picture of outbreak dynamics~\cite{grenfell2004, attwood2022, silva2023, burki2024}. Therefore, several approaches to digital collaborative surveillance have been proposed. In particular, notable efforts have been dedicated to platforms for sharing data and bioinformatics toolkits, such as GISAID~\cite{shu2017} and Nextstrain~\cite{hadfield2018} for viral genomes, and Freyja 2~\cite{levy2025} for multi-pathogen wastewater sampling. Other research has highlighted the importance of \gls{ai} for modelling epidemics~\cite{kraemer2025}, as well as the need to openly release and share such models. 

Currently, most surveillance strategies are based on the collection of pathogenic genomes, which provide a wealth of information on evolution, transmission, and pathogenicity (e.g., severity, escape from immunity, drug sensitivity)~\cite{robishaw2021, djordjevic2024}. Whereas pathogenicity assays tend to rely on biochemical experiments and clinical trials, data-driven modelling approaches based on \textit{phylogenetics}~\cite{grenfell2004, biek2015} and networks~\cite{matteson2023} may enhance our comprehension of how pathogens evolve and transmit within human populations. However, genomic data are only a part of the pandemic preparedness puzzle. For instance, in light of the One Health framework, animal reservoir monitoring is poised to play a critical role for preparedness. In these scenarios, large-scale sequencing is, in addition to substantial personnel and material costs, inherently more difficult as reservoirs may be delicate or difficult to access. Building on wildlife conservation strategies~\cite{tuia2022}, sensor-based technologies constitute a promising avenue~\cite{watsa2020, barroso2023} for noninvasive reservoir monitoring. More specifically, video imagery holds potential, using remote sensors (satellites, unmanned aerial vehicles) to study herd dynamics or local camera systems to monitor behavioural patterns within animal groups~\cite{dawkins2003} (see Section~\ref{sec:bkg_animal_welfare}).

Consequently, modern preparedness is inherently data-heavy and multifaceted, requiring interoperable frameworks for sharing datasets, \gls{ai} models, and analytical toolkits. The integration of heterogeneous data streams from DNA sequences to videos defines the next generation of pandemic resilience.

\section{A primer on phylogenetics}
\label{sec:bkg_phylo}
The overarching goal of modern phylogenetics is to model how evolutionary units change and diversify (termed \emph{taxa} in biology). Thus, the field is a fundamental component of biomedicine and pandemic preparedness, as it contributes to our understanding of how pathogens originate, evolve, and spread~\cite{eickmann2003, dudas2017, attwood2022}. Although the overwhelming majority of phylogenetic studies pertain to biological problems \cite{felsenstein2004, yang2014, stadler2024}, even linguists leverage phylogenetic techniques to study languages as evolutionary units~\cite{greenhill2023, heggarty2023}. The granularity of units may range from the individual level to any taxonomic rank. The central object of phylogenetics is the \emph{phylogenetic tree}, a hierarchical branching diagram that depicts the evolutionary history of the observed entities. \textit{Phylogenetic networks} are a superset of phylogenetic trees that are notably useful when reticulate evolutionary processes such as horizontal gene transfer are in play~\cite{huson2006, stadler2024}.

Phylogenetic trees are traditionally inferred by comparing data from observable entities of interest. Historically, this has relied on qualitative comparisons of phenotypic characteristics, such as morphology~\cite{dietrich1998}. However, technological advances in computer science and genetic sequencing~\cite{sanger1959, sanger1977} have ushered in a new era of objective, molecular data-driven phylogenetics~\cite{michener1957, zuckerkandl1962, zuckerkandl1965, fitch1967}.

In this section, I provide a short primer on key phylogenetic concepts, with a note on protein structure-based phylogenetics, which may prove to be a useful complement to sequence-based analyses that are currently the norm in the field.

\subsection{Phylogenetic trees}

From a computer science perspective, a phylogenetic tree (Fig.~\ref{fig:basictree}) consists of a set of nodes connected by edges (also called \emph{branches}) that form an acyclic graph in which any two nodes are connected by exactly one path. Nodes can be divided into two categories. \emph{Leaf nodes}, also called \emph{tips} or \emph{terminal nodes,} are nodes that do not have any children/descendants. They are connected to only one neighbour: their most recent ancestor. A \emph{cherry} denotes a pair of leaves that share the same parent. Conversely, nodes that have at least one descendant are called \emph{internal} or \emph{ancestral nodes}. Their placement is usually inferred from observable leaf data. From a biological perspective, \emph{leaf} nodes correspond to observed taxa, while \textit{internal} nodes constitute the \gls{mrca} of all taxa that descend from them. They are not observable in the present state.

A tree is said to be \textit{labelled} if its leaf nodes are assigned to a specific entity. A tree with no information on labels and branch lengths is called a \textit{topology}, as it only contains information on the connectedness between the different nodes.

A tree is said to be \emph{rooted} if it satisfies the following two conditions. First, the edges are directed from the parent to the child. Second, the tree includes a node called the \emph{root}, which serves as the \gls{mrca} of all taxa of interest. A tree where edges are undirected (or bidirectional) and no root exists is called \textit{unrooted}.

A rooted tree is said to be \textit{ultrametric} if the distance from each leaf to the root is constant.

A tree is said to be \textit{binary} or \textit{bifurcating} if each internal node is connected to exactly two children nodes. In other words, leaves are of degree 1, and internal nodes are of degree 3 (two children and one parent), except for the root for rooted trees which is of degree 2 (only two children). It is the easiest (and most common) form of tree used in phylogenetics.

More generally, a tree is said to be \textit{n-ary} if each internal node is connected to \textit{at most} $n$ children. The edge cases include $n = 0$ (a single point) and $n = 1$, a straight line. In phylogenetics, an internal node with more than two children is called a \textit{polytomy}.

A key challenge in phylogenetic inference is the vastness of tree space. For a problem set with $n$ taxa, there are $1\cdot3\cdot5\cdot\ldots\cdot (2n-5) \cdot (2n-3) = (2n-3)!!$ binary labelled trees~\cite{cavallisforza1967} ($!!$ denotes the \emph{semifactorial} operator). For $n$ = 5 taxa, there are 105 combinations; for $n$ = 53 taxa, 2.84$\times$10$^{82}$ combinations, a number exceeding estimates of the number of protons in the observable universe~\cite{eddington1946}. Thus, we need efficient data structures to store and compare trees and algorithms to sample trees and perform optimisation operations.

\subsection{Representing trees}

In computer science, traditional ways to represent trees include linked lists or recursive classes using a node class with attributes for data and references to its children~\cite{knuth1997}. Various methods have been proposed to compress a tree with $n$ leaves into a single-integer representation through counting methods~\cite{knuth1973, rotem1978, proskurowski1980, rohlf1983, palacios2022}. Although such methods allow for a compact representation of trees, they are generally impractical for real-world phylogenetic applications and lack human readability.

In the 1990s, two tree representations were proposed for phylogenetic applications: pair matching~\cite{diaconis1998} and the Newick format (Fig.~\ref{fig:basictree})~\cite{olsen1990, felsenstein2004}, the latter of which has since become the \textit{de facto} standard in phylogenetic software. The Newick format represents trees as a set of nested parentheses that enclose pairs of nodes or, by recursion, subtrees. The main advantages of the Newick format lie in its simplicity and flexibility, as it can support various annotations, such as branch lengths and uncertainty measures. Whilst relatively human-readable for small trees, Newick strings become difficult to visually parse and compare for larger numbers of taxa. Importantly, the Newick format is not \emph{bijective} to the space of (binary) trees, as isomorphisms can be constructed by permuting the order of nodes or subtrees within a pair. In addition, sampling random trees using the Newick format is not trivial.

A desirable final object to encode trees is a vector, i.e., a one-dimensional array structure. It is one of the most commonly used, flexible, and memory-efficient objects for storing data in many programming languages~\cite{stroustrup2013, harris2020, cormen2022}. A few vector-based representations have been proposed in previous studies, including graph polynomials~\cite{liu2021, liu2022} and the compact bijective ladderized vector (CBLV)~\cite{voznica2022}. However, these formats have not been tailored for tree sampling or phylogenetic inference, as graph polynomials were designed for tree clustering and phylogenetic model selection~\cite{liu2021, liu2022}, whereas the CBLV format was implemented for deep learning-based inference of phylogenetic model parameters~\cite{voznica2022}. Finally, although ACOPHY \cite{dinh2010} shares some similarities with phylo2vec with respect to its encoding strategy, its primary use case remains ant colony optimisation.

\subsection{Phylogenetic inference}
\label{sec:bkg_phylo_inference}

At its core, phylogenetic inference aims to estimate a (binary) phylogenetic tree $T$ for $n$ entities or taxa using comparable data. An intuitive procedure for inferring such a tree is to calculate the distances between the data at hand. For instance, a naive approach is to build a distance matrix of taxa by comparing the presence/absence of certain characters (or sequences) using a Hamming distance ($d(u,v) = \sum_i 1_{u_i \neq v_i}$). From this distance matrix, methods based on hierarchical clustering techniques such as the unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA)~\cite{sokal1958} or the \gls{nj}~\cite{saitou1987, lefort2015} method can be employed to infer a tree. However, the advent of molecular evolutionary theory~\cite{zuckerkandl1965, fitch1967, kimura1968, ohta1992} propelled the development of new statistical frameworks for phylogenetic inference, chief among which is maximum likelihood estimation~\cite{felsenstein1981}. The overarching goal of this framework is to find model parameters $\Theta$ that maximise the \emph{phylogenetic likelihood} of given character data $D$ (e.g., nucleotide bases or amino acid residues) as follows: $max_\Theta P(D|\Theta)$. The overwhelming majority of maximum likelihood frameworks use Felsenstein's likelihood~\cite{felsenstein1981}, which uses an efficient dynamic programming algorithm to recursively calculate the likelihood of subtrees through a post-order traversal. In this framework, model parameters include the tree topology, branch lengths, and a \emph{substitution model} of evolution to describe the probabilities of changes in the (homologous) characters of interest~\cite{duchene2024}. In DNA, notable substitution models are the naive Jukes-Cantor model~\cite{jukes1969}, which assumes that all substitutions occur uniformly, and the flexible general time-reversible (GTR) model~\cite{tavare1986}, which allows each type of substitution to have its own rate and accounts for unequal base frequencies. For protein-based analyses, substitution models are often empirically derived from large homologous protein alignments. Examples include the WAG~\cite{whelan2001} and LG~\cite{le2008b} models.
 
Practically, maximum likelihood–based algorithms can be implemented by starting from a sensible initial tree (e.g., using \gls{nj}) and iteratively optimising it through successive tree rearrangements. These can be very local, such as \gls{nni}, or more extensive, such as \gls{spr} and \gls{tbr}. Popular software for maximum likelihood-based phylogenetics include PhyML~\cite{guindon2003}, IQ-TREE~\cite{minh2020}, and RAxML-NG~\cite{kozlov2019}. Alternatively, Bayesian inference relies on \gls{mcmc} to infer a posterior distribution over trees and other model parameters given the observed data~\cite{huelsenbeck2001, drummond2015}. This approach not only yields point estimates but also provides measures of uncertainty, allowing for more robust and reliable evolutionary inference. Compared to maximum likelihood-based approaches, Bayesian methods require specifying prior distributions on model parameters and tree topologies, and \gls{mcmc} convergence must be carefully assessed to ensure adequate sampling of the posterior. These methods are particularly powerful for estimating divergence times under molecular clock~\cite{zuckerkandl1962, zuckerkandl1965} models. Widely used software packages include MrBayes~\cite{huelsenbeck2001} and BEAST~\cite{bouckaert2014, baele2025}.

\subsection{Phylogenetic mixture modelling}

Maximum likelihood-based frameworks generally strive to infer a single optimal tree from observed molecular or morphological data. In molecular phylogenetics, this assumption may be too strict in some cases~\cite{edwards2009, wong2024}. For instance, genomes typically consist of a multitude of loci which have undergone vastly different evolutionary processes. In particular, incomplete lineage sorting, horizontal gene transfer, and recombination are common sources of gene tree heterogeneity that can lead to conflicting phylogenetic signals in different genomic regions. A typical example is that of coronaviruses, whose genomes contain multiple signatures of recombination events, which are believed to drive spillover events~\cite{wells2023}. Although historical approaches valued the “total evidence” mantra~\cite{kluge1989}, that is, maximising the amount of information to base a phylogenetic analysis, such concatenation methods can be problematic. In this framework, heterogeneous phylogenetic signals are averaged, potentially obscuring true evolutionary relationships or introducing systematic biases. In that light, a developing body of research has strived to allow for heterogeneity between trees inferred at the genic level~\cite{edwards2009}. Methods based on the multi-species coalescent have allowed the inference of species-level trees or networks in scenarios where incomplete lineage sorting is present~\cite{rannala2003, mirarab2021, zhang2022}. Alternatively, mixture modelling-based approaches~\cite{pagel2005, boussau2009, crotty2019, wong2024} are useful for studying recombination-imprinted evolution by allowing the existence of multiple possible phylogenetic trees to represent an alignment of interest. In particular, the recent \gls{mast} model~\cite{wong2024} relaxes the assumption of a single tree explaining the evolutionary history across a selected genetic alignment but allows for multiple trees to coexist and explain various parts of the alignment of interest. Drawing from Boussau \textit{et al.}~\cite{boussau2009}, \gls{mast} employs the expectation-maximisation algorithm to calculate the expected likelihood (E step) and optimise estimates of various phylogenetic parameters (branch lengths, substitution rate parameters, nucleotide frequency parameters) (M step), before classifying sites into different \emph{mixture} (or \emph{classes}). Such multi-tree models are especially useful for understanding the evolution of viruses such as coronaviruses, where a number of recombination events are in play~\cite{wells2023}. 

\subsection{Protein structural phylogenetics}

The popularity of sequence-based phylogenetics relies on the spectacular progress in data availability through rapid sequencing and software that leverage robust statistical frameworks. Nevertheless, an emerging body of research is exploring the feasibility and utility of protein structural data in phylogenetic studies. Although largely predetermined by the underlying chain of amino acids~\cite{anfinsen1973}, the structure of a protein is fundamental to its function. In addition, protein structures have been shown to be more conserved than amino acid sequences, especially within core regions~\cite{wood1999, reeves2006, illergaard2009}, where vastly different genes in terms of structure may have extremely homologous structures~\cite{moi2022}. Finally, the emergence of protein structure models such as AlphaFold~\cite{jumper2021, abramson2024}, ColabFold~\cite{mirdita2022} and ESM~\cite{lin2023, hayes2025} have contributed to the release of atlases of protein structures~\cite{varadi2022, lin2023, kim2025b}, which compared to sequences, remain experimentally time- and cost-intensive to produce~\cite{schwede2013}. Thus, structure-based phylogenetics has garnered significant interest for revealing functional insights and resolving evolutionary relationships beyond the “twilight zone” of sequence identity~\cite{rost1999}.

Nevertheless, comparing continuous, three-dimensional, protein structures for phylogenetic analysis is less obvious than discrete, linear sequences. A substantial body of research has been dedicated to developing structure comparison metrics for distance-based phylogenetics, such as SSM~\cite{krissinel2004} and TM-Align~\cite{zhang2005}. Recently, the deep learning revolution~\cite{lecun2015} has propelled new avenues for structure-based phylogenetics. On the one hand, embeddings from protein language models (PLMs; e.g., ESM~\cite{lin2023, hayes2025}, MSA Transformer~\cite{rao2021}) have been shown to encode some level of phylogenetic information~\cite{lupo2022, tule2025}. However, PLMs are currently of limited precision out of the box because of the coarseness of their training set~\cite{gomez2024}. This makes them impractical for pandemic-related phylogenetics, as analyses usually involve comparing variants that differ by only a few mutations~\cite{markov2023}. Another avenue consists of discretising experimentally derived protein structures into a finite set of characters to be used downstream for maximum likelihood-based inference. A notable contribution is the vector quantized variational autoencoder (VQ-VAE) model developed within the Foldseek toolkit~\cite{vankempen2024}, whose encoder maps each residue in a structure to one of 20 discrete characters using geometrical features (e.g., torsion angles and distances between $C_\alpha$ atoms) calculated using its nearest neighbour. Originally developed for protein structure search and clustering, the 3Di alphabet has been used for phylogenetic analyses of diverse protein families (e.g. ferritins~\cite{puente2023}, RRNPPA quorum sensing receptors~\cite{moi2023}, and histones~\cite{fullmer2025}). However, to the best of our knowledge, this model has seldom been employed for pathogenic protein phylogenetics. A notable example is Mifsud \textit{et al.}~\cite{mifsud2024}, who used model-driven protein structures to study the deep history of the \textit{Flaviviridae} family. Compared to model-predicted structure atlases, experimental structure datasets are typically more fine-grained but may be noisier. Taking the example of spike protein structures from betacoronaviruses, experimental structures from \gls{pdb} may exhibit significant variation with respect to quality metrics such as clashes or sidechain outliers \cite{gore2017}. In addition, their diversity makes it more difficult to establish a “consensus” structure for a given species or variant, as structures may vary with the systems in which they are expressed and the ligands to which they are bound.

\section{Betacoronaviruses: virology and evolution}

\textit{Betacoronavirus} is a monophyletic genus of the coronavirus subfamily (\textit{Orthocoronavirinae}) with five subgenera: \textit{Embecovirus} (e.g., HCoV-OC43), \textit{Hibecovirus}, \textit{Merbecovirus} (e.g., MERS-CoV), \textit{Nobecovirus}, \textit{Sarbecovirus} (e.g., SARS-CoV)~\cite{woo2023}. Like other coronaviruses, betacoronaviruses are single-stranded, positive-sense (i.e., their RNA genome can be directly translated into proteins) and enveloped (i.e., they contain an outer layer consisting of a lipid membrane and surface viral proteins). They are of particular relevance to public health, as they have triggered three major outbreaks in the 21st century: SARS~\cite{ksiazek2003, drosten2003, rota2003}, MERS~\cite{zaki2012}, and COVID-19~\cite{huang2020, zhu2020}, and regular common cold outbreaks in humans via HCoV-HKU1 and HCoV-OC43 viruses~\cite{jackson2022, woo2023}. Viruses in this group can infect a host of mammalian species~\cite{woo2023}. Bats and rodents constitute natural reservoirs~\cite{li2005, ruiz-aravena2022, wang2020}, and zoonosis is common to humans via diverse intermediates, including swine~\cite{cui2019}, civets~\cite{ge2013}, and camels~\cite{azhar2014}, among others. Understanding cross-species transmission of \textit{Betacoronavirus} from its natural reservoirs to other mammals is therefore essential for preventing future outbreaks in humans and other vulnerable mammals.

\input{fig/chimera}

All betacoronaviruses have relatively large genomes at the scale of RNA viruses (27-32 kb) and encode four major structural proteins: the nucleocapsid (N), located within the virion, and the membrane-associated envelope (E), membrane (M), and spike (S) proteins~\cite{flint2020, woo2023}, the latter being paramount for mediating viral entry in host cells. Spikes are large (1200-1400 residues) petal-shaped glycoproteins (Fig.~\ref{fig:bcov_spike}) with two major subunits: the S1 subunit, which mediates receptor binding, and the S2 subunit, which mediates membrane fusion~\cite{jackson2022}. The S1 subunit typically contains a receptor-binding domain in its C-terminal region (murine coronaviruses may have it in their N-terminal region~\cite{belouzard2012}) which binds to mammalian receptors. The most notable receptor is human \gls{ace2}, a key enzyme in the blood pressure-regulating renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, which is targeted by the pandemic-triggering SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 viruses~\cite{starr2022, lan2020, hoffmann2020}.

One of the key mechanisms mediating host tropism in coronaviruses is recombination~\cite{bobay2020, turakhia2022}. However, its influence remains disputed~\cite{wells2023}. Evidence from the SARS and MERS outbreaks has illustrated that the use of the human receptors ACE2 and DPP4 is likely to have arisen from recombination processes~\cite{anthony2017, wells2021, wells2023}, but not for SARS-CoV-2~\cite{boni2020, wrobel2021, wells2021, starr2022, temmam2022, wells2023}. More generally, the spike glycoprotein is considered a general hotspot for recombination, although several regions, such as the N-terminal domain of the S1 subunit, have been found to be shaped more by gradual mutation~\cite{deklerk2022}. To this end, leveraging protein structural data for phylogenetic analysis may provide new insights into the evolutionary dynamics of \textit{Betacoronavirus} spike proteins.

\section{Animal surveillance for pandemic preparedness}
\label{sec:bkg_animal_welfare}

Most recent pandemics have been of zoonotic origin, with pathogens from wildlife or livestock spilling over into human populations~\cite{vora2022}. The scale and poor accessibility of animal reservoirs make high-coverage genetic surveillance economically and logistically impractical~\cite{tixier2009, astill2018, plowright2019, giles2021}. In addition, systematic sampling and genomic sequencing remain time-intensive and lead to non-negligible delays in infection response, which may be critical in the context of rapidly spreading infections. Thus, there is a strong demand for monitoring methods that can detect infection or welfare impairments while minimising experimenter intervention, impediments to animal behaviour, and delays from data collection to interpretation.

\subsection{Poultry farming as a case study for early detection of infection in animals}

With a third of US human cases being exposed to poultry-related operations as of August 2025~\cite{cdc2025}, the current outbreak of H5N1 influenza~\cite{xie2023} has highlighted the importance of poultry livestock as a pressure point for pandemic preparedness. Indeed, poultry birds constitute potential vectors of pathogens such as influenza~\cite{capua2006, swayne2025}, owing to their interactions with wild flocks which typically serve as natural hosts. Although spillover to humans remains rare~\cite{swayne2025}, such transmission events can occur when infected livestock animals come into close contact with humans. Furthermore, poultry farming is critical to food security as the primary and fastest increasing meat source for human consumption, accounting for 40\% of the world's meat production~\cite{oecd2025}. Thus, it is imperative to detect infection in both wild and livestock herds early to ensure a stable supply and prevent the risk of spillover into human populations.  

\subsubsection{\textit{E. coli} as a model infection in broiler chickens}

One of the major diseases of concern in poultry farming is Avian Pathogenic \textit{Escherichia coli (APEC)}~\cite{nolan2020}. Although typically found in the gastrointestinal tract of many mammals, birds, and reptiles~\cite{tenaillon2010}, pathogenic strains of \textit{E. coli} are increasingly prevalent, particularly in Nordic poultry farms~\cite{ronco2017, kromann2023}. APEC is a multi-systemic infection capable of causing cellulitis~\cite{poulsen2018}, respiratory tract infections (pharyngitis, airsacculitis), and sepsis~\cite{dho1999, stromberg2017}. Phylogenetic analyses of pathogenic \textit{Escherichia coli} serotypes have found strong similarities between human and avian strains and suggested that APEC may have zoonotic potential and implications for both animal and human health~\cite{moulin2007, stromberg2017}.

\subsection{Computer vision for animal welfare monitoring}

Current methods for wildlife and livestock monitoring involve the collection of audio, radio, and video signals~\cite{tuia2022}. Among these three data streams, videography offers a suitable trade-off between precision, invasiveness, and scalability. RFID or GPS sensors are invasive and difficult to scale, whereas bioacoustic sensors offer low precision at the individual level. Nonetheless, extracting meaningful insights from extensive video datasets requires cutting-edge computer vision methods. A successful research avenue has focused on estimating optical flow, the apparent motion of pixels between consecutive frames~\cite{szeliski2022} (see Fig.~\ref{fig:of_diagram}), to assess welfare in chickens~\cite{dawkins2012, colles2016, dawkins2017, penafernandez2018, dawkins2021, gebhardt2021}. Indeed, continuous optical flow data obtained from CCTV footage correlated with flock-level welfare impairments linked to \textit{Campylobacter jejuni} (\textit{C. jejuni})~\cite{colles2016}, footpad dermatitis, and hock burn~\cite{dawkins2017}. However, optical flow has limited precision for such analyses, as it does not provide information at the level of objects or individuals (only at the level of pixels). Moreover, despite recent progress in deep learning-based methods~\cite{dosovitskiy2015, chen2016, hur2020, teed2020, wang2025}, optical flow data remain sensitive to occlusions and noise (e.g., light variations, shadows, jitter). % Thus, in these studies, the per-pixel flow estimates were aggregated at a flock level to assess welfare trends.

Over the past decade, the deep learning revolution~\cite{lecun2015} has catalysed the production of a plethora of data-driven computer vision methods for object recognition, pose estimation, and tracking~\cite{zhao2019, zheng2023}. These approaches have gained substantial traction in ecology~\cite{brown2018} and behavioural neuroscience~\cite{mathis2020} as a means of objectively quantifying animal behaviour~\cite{anderson2014}. One of the most popular methods is the \gls{dlc}~\cite{mathis2018}, which has greatly contributed to the democratisation of tracking and pose estimation in life sciences by minimising the need for time-consuming manual annotations to achieve suitable performance. However, the potential of these methods for large-cohort experiments, such as in poultry farms, remains relatively unexplored, as tracking becomes more computationally intensive and challenging due to occlusions~\cite{zhou2023, han2025}. Several studies have demonstrated the feasibility of detecting infection in broiler chickens with a few animals~\cite{zhuang2019} or in controlled settings, where cameras do not capture freely roaming animals~\cite{doornweerd2021}. Since our initial study~\cite{scheidwasser2025c}, a wide variety of methods have been proposed~\cite{depuru2024,ehsan2024,yang2024}, but they do not yet address early infection detection. Importantly, video data and models for these studies are rarely openly available, making comparison and reproducibility difficult.
