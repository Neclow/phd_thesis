In this thesis, I present three studies pertaining to diverse, yet related, facets of pandemic preparedness. Engineering new data formats that maximise the value of lesser-explored datasets can complement existing analysis frameworks for understanding the emergence, transmission, and evolution of pathogens with pandemic potential.

The first study describes the implementation of phylo2vec~\cite{penn2025}, a vector-based representation of binary trees designed to summarise vast phylogeny datasets and bridge the worlds of phylogenetics and machine/deep learning. Drawing inspiration from tree enumeration strategies~\cite{rohlf1983}, this study propelled the development of vector-based representations of phylogenies, such as HOP~\cite{chauve2025} and OLA~\cite{richman2025}.

Nevertheless, the path to the usage of these streamlined data types in relevant machine or deep learning applications remains in its early stages. More generally, phylogenetic inference techniques have seen little impact from modern deep learning techniques~\cite{sapoval2022}. At its essence, deep learning requires a neural network and data from which the network learns, as well as a metric to evaluate the learning performance~\cite{lecun2015}. When modelling maximum likelihood-based phylogenetic inference with deep learning, input data would typically include an alignment of $n$ molecular sequences with $m$ sites and a model of evolution. In most likelihood-based frameworks, inference outputs are a single bifurcating tree and associated parameters that maximise Felsenstein's likelihood~\cite{felsenstein1973, felsenstein1981}. While potentially adaptable to deep learning-based applications, Felsenstein's likelihood is not fully differentiable due to the discrete nature of tree topologies. To this end, several studies have developed continuous relaxations of tree space or projections in non-Euclidean spaces~\cite{penn2023, mimori2023, macaulay2024}. However, current libraries for gradient computations on tree likelihood remain less efficient than precise handcrafted algorithms~\cite{fourment2023}. Among other factors, these libraries have not yet fully leveraged graphics processing units (GPUs) to parallelise computations~\cite{fourment2023}, a hallmark of the deep learning revolution~\cite{krizhevsky2012, lecun2015, vaswani2017}.

Beyond issues related to tree likelihood, building comprehensive and biologically plausible training datasets is also non-trivial. In general, the validity of an inferred tree is inherently constrained by available data and models of evolution~\cite{yang2014}. To circumvent this issue, Nesterenko \textit{et al.}~\cite{nesterenko2025} used simulated data from trees for distance-based phylogenetic inference using a Transformer model, but the model did not outperform state-of-the-art likelihood-based methods. Moreover, in cases involving reticulate processes (e.g. recombination), binary trees may provide an incomplete picture of the evolutionary history~\cite{huson2006, wong2024}.

Last, but most prominently, a deep learning-based solution would need to be both sufficiently flexible and scalable to incorporate a highly variable-length input and predict an optimal tree from a combinatorially vast tree space~\cite{sapoval2022}. Various sequence representation approaches have been explored, including chaos game representations~\cite{jeffrey1990} and learnable representations from Transformer-based models~\cite{consens2025}. However, these approaches have yet to provide additional insights into evolutionary questions~\cite{ektefaie2025}. Alternatively, graph-based generative models may also have the potential to generate plausible trees~\cite{xie2023b, xie2025}, but currently remain limited for practical phylogenetic applications. Consequently, an emerging body of research at the intersection of deep learning and phylogenetics has focused on optimising model parameters through tools such as ModelTeller~\cite{abadi2020} and PhyloDeep~\cite{voznica2022}, which constitutes a promising avenue for phylo2vec usage. Overall, phylo2vec representations are technically amenable to providing practical value for various phylogenetic inference tasks, with broader adoption primarily contingent on dissemination and integration into existing phylogenetic workflows. %Taken together, while deep learning-based phylogenetic inference is computationally and theoretically challenging, auxiliary tasks such as evolutionary model selection offer opportunities where phylo2vec representations can provide practical value.

Finally, the development of phylo2vec also motivated the implementation of an eponymous high-performance software package for phylogenetic tree manipulation~\cite{scheidwasser2025}. The current version of the library comprises various functions for tree sampling, tree format conversion, and other operations on trees. However, the development of key features such as inference models represents an important next step toward spearheading adoption in the bioinformatics community. Whereas the computational efficiency of the library may facilitate rapid tree visualisation, the non-equivalence of topological moves in the phylo2vec space to rooted \gls{spr} moves presents a significant opportunity for advancing phylogenetic inference methods based on phylo2vec-derived representations.

The second study of this thesis aimed to use protein structure information to analyse the evolutionary determinants of \textit{Betacoronavirus} spike proteins. Through multi-tree mixture modelling~\cite{wong2024}, we propose that recombination in the S1 subunit and gradual changes in the \gls{ntd} domain mainly act as upstream factors influencing receptor usage. These processes alone do not cause major structural shifts in the spike glycoprotein~\cite{scheidwasser2025a}. This finding aligns with previous work supporting the role of \gls{ntd} in shaping \gls{ace2} affinity~\cite{meng2022}, and that recombination, while a prominent mechanism underpinning spike evolution~\cite{li2020}, is not a major driver of \gls{ace2} binding in betacoronaviruses~\cite{wrobel2021, boni2020, wells2023, starr2022}.

Although recent breakthroughs in protein structure modelling powered by deep learning~\cite{lin2023, jumper2021, abramson2024, mirdita2022} have spearheaded the development of large-scale structural datasets~\cite{varadi2022, kim2025b, vankempen2024}, these models may struggle with fine-grained variants that differ subtly from their training data~\cite{gomez2024}. This limitation, combined with the computational cost of folding thousands of structures, led us to use experimentally derived structures from the \gls{pdb}~\cite{berman2000, rose2010}. However, experimental structures present their own challenges. Variability arises from differences in resolution, expression systems, and ligand-induced conformational changes, all of which may complicate direct comparisons~\cite{pronker2023, hasse2024}. For viral protein structure analyses, future research should explore the applicability of topological deep learning~\cite{wee2025} for precise variant prediction or consider fine-tuning existing models on more detailed viral datasets~\cite{kim2025b}.

The recent emergence of protein structural phylogenetic studies~\cite{moi2023, puente2023, puente-lelievre2025, fullmer2025, garg2025, mutti2025, mifsud2024, mifsud2025} stems from the development of Foldseek's 3Di alphabet~\cite{vankempen2024}, which enables mapping continuous structures to discrete characters, making structures amenable for maximum-likelihood phylogenetics. Although this model has helped provide new insights into structural evolution, several limitations exist. First, its utility has been disputed in alpha helix-rich domains~\cite{fullmer2025}. This has been further evidenced in this study through mixture modelling ambiguities in our phylogenetic analysis of the S2 subunit, whose heptad regions typically form helix bundles in the prefusion stage~\cite{li2016, cai2020}. Second, most structural phylogenetics research targets deep evolutionary relationships in and beyond the ``twilight zone" of sequence similarity~\cite{rost1999}. Conversely, \textit{Betacoronavirus} diversification occurs over much shorter timescales~\cite{markov2023}. For such scenarios, 3Di-based phylogenies have been shown to offer reduced precision~\cite{puente2023, puente-lelievre2025}. From the results of our study, we argue that our results, although not encompassing genome-scale alignments, provide valuable insights into how recombination and mutation shape the evolution of spike protein-binding domains. Together, these limitations highlight the need for further methodological development of structure discretisation methods. 

The analyses presented here can be extended in several directions to offer additional insights into the evolution of \textit{Betacoronavirus} spike glycoproteins. Because experimentally solved structures are biased toward well-studied variants~\cite{scheidwasser2025a} (e.g., SARS-CoV-2 lineages), we selected a limited number of samples per strain to reduce bias in the phylogenetic mixture models. Second, our mixture modelling approach remains tractable in that all trees share the same model of evolution and rate parameters~\cite{wong2024}. Modelling with higher complexity can lead to numerical optimisation problems but may reveal further evolutionary phenomena, notably in the S2 subunit. Whilst edge-unlinked models have been proposed as an alternative~\cite{mutti2025}, we opted against this approach due to concerns about over-parametrisation and convergence difficulties given our small dataset size. Finally, our study is inherently centred on a spike-centric lens of \textit{Betacoronavirus} evolution. Although a natural starting point as a recombination hotspot and its key function in mediating host tropism~\cite{wells2023}, analysing other structural proteins may help capture a more general view of \textit{Betacoronavirus} evolution nd potentially unlock new insights into their remarkable host tropism flexibility. % Finally, our study is inherently centred on a spike-centric lens of \textit{Betacoronavirus} evolution and may offer limited insight into their general evolution. While spike glycoproteins are a natural starting point for exploring the evolutionary dynamics of coronaviruses, extending the analysis to other structural proteins may provide a broader perspective and reveal additional mechanisms underlying their flexibility and mammalian host range.

The final study of this thesis focused on the early prediction of infection in broiler chickens by monitoring behaviour from video recordings using markerless tracking. We successfully tracked the chickens by fine-tuning a multi-animal model within the DeepLabCut toolkit~\cite{mathis2018, lauer2022}. Despite using only 100 manually labelled frames for fine-tuning, tracking performance remained adequate on unlabelled video frames, partly due to the high homogeneity of the dataset (same breed, similar pens, and consistent camera angles). Nevertheless, this required substantial manual effort, totalling 20,000 labelled data points (100 $\times$ 5 key points per animal $\times$ 20 animals per room $\times$ 2 rooms). Future studies should prioritise developing general-purpose datasets for broiler chickens in indoor, free-ranging environments to advance research in this area. More advanced frameworks, such as SuperAnimal~\cite{ye2024}, although currently limited to quadrupedal species, may also prove valuable for developing foundation models for avian tracking. Importantly, future models should not only be able to track accurately, but also detect physical patterns (e.g., ruffled feathers, excessive scratching) that could signal illness or compromised welfare. Leveraging other data streams, such as animal pose and audio signals, may provide valuable information for achieving this task, which may require additional data labelling or unsupervised techniques to distinguish behavioural patterns~\cite{todd2017, gunel2019}.

In this study however, tracking information was sufficient to extract several behavioural feature vectors related to locomotion and feeding. Reduced feeding and decreased locomotion, were also observed in previous studies examining the effects of \textit{E. coli} endotoxins in broilers~\cite{johnson1993, cheng2004, gregory2009}. Additionally, general reluctance to stand was previously observed in broilers infected subcutaneously with \textit{E. coli}, causing further dermatological symptoms~\cite{jonare2024}. A variety of relevant behavioural features could be added to establish more comprehensive behavioural profiles of the study population. For instance, although heavily correlated with feed usage and body weight, water consumption monitoring has proven useful for assessing welfare in broilers~\cite{manning2007}. Furthermore, analysing social behaviour may serve as a useful proxy for disease prediction. Chickens typically exert diverse social and gregarious behaviours, some of which contribute to alleviating pathogenic transmission. A typical example is allopreening (cleaning or grooming feathers of conspecifics) which helps remove external parasites~\cite{bush2018, jackson2025}.

A major difficulty of studies of this kind is the preservation of animal identity across videos, precluding within-subject effects and restricting the analysis to group-wise statistical comparisons. Two solutions hold potential to address this challenge. First, animal marking using paint marks or wing tags may facilitate identity preservation without changing the data collection design. Nonetheless, this may disturb natural behaviour and is not readily scalable to large-scale settings. Second, continuous monitoring using closed-circuit television (CCTV) may address this issue by generating a single video stream across the lifetime of birds instead of temporally fragmented snapshots. Should video data need to be stored, a trade-off between duration and quality must be struck to address data storage constraints. A high-quality video recording with a duration of one hour and a standard bit rate ($\approx$ four megabytes per second (Mbps)~\cite{hikvision2015}) would require approximately 2–4 gigabytes (GB) of storage per hour of video, or 1.5–3 terabytes (TB) for 35 days (the average time for Ross 308 broilers to reach slaughter weight~\cite{ross308, rspca2020}). Consequently, real-time tracking has the potential to alleviate these storage demands while reducing risks associated with data ownership, privacy, and security~\cite{wiseman2019, tzachor2022}. Federated learning may further help build generalisable and robust foundational models without compromising data privacy~\cite{khurana2025}. Releasing reproducible and openly accessible protocols is also paramount to guarantee transparency, as well as a fair distribution of benefits within the agricultural community~\cite{wiseman2019}. 

A further limitation of this study is its scalability to larger settings (e.g., poultry farms). Indeed, monitoring hundreds or thousands of extremely similar animals remains computationally and logistically challenging. In the short run, optical flow-based methods may be preferable for large flocks, as they provide flock-level welfare assessment that aligns with how birds are currently managed in commercial settings~\cite{dawkins2025}. However, as precision livestock farming advances, individual-level tracking may become increasingly valuable for targeted interventions, creating a trade-off between behavioural resolution and flock-scale inference. Therefore, I believe that a hybrid system combining optical flow analysis with targeted individual tracking could constitute an effective compromise, combining flock-level measures with individual-level measurements. To achieve this, a dual-camera system could be implemented. First, a low-cost camera would perform continuous monitoring of regions with abnormal activity using optical flow. Second, a high-quality camera would be used for animal tracking within the regions flagged by the first system, informing the farmer or experimenter of potentially anomalous individuals. Consequently, future research should aim to validate this framework through large-scale experiments incorporating diverse poultry-affecting pathogens across multiple replication phases, with all data and code made openly available to facilitate reproducibility and advance the fields of animal welfare monitoring and precision livestock farming.

A final conceptual limitation concerns whether the early detection of infection in broiler chickens or wildlife sanctuaries could entirely prevent mass culling. Demonstrating with high confidence that infection is confined to part of a flock remains extremely difficult, limiting the feasibility of reducing culling on this basis. Nonetheless, this study may still provide an avenue for making laboratory testing more targeted. By integrating feedback from \gls{ai}-based tracking, pathogen sampling can occur earlier, mitigating the need to wait for widespread mortality and severe welfare impacts.

In conclusion, this thesis demonstrates that pandemic preparedness may be enhanced by transformative approaches to phylogenetic data engineering and cross-disciplinary integration of new data streams such as protein structure and animal behaviour. Through three studies that span data types used in pandemic preparedness, I illustrated how novel data representations and computational methodologies can address critical gaps in pathogen monitoring and disease prediction systems. First, the development of phylo2vec established a foundational framework for bridging phylogenetics with machine learning through efficient vectorisation of evolutionary trees, facilitating rapid computational analysis of pathogen transmission dynamics. Complementing this genomic perspective, the second study showed the potential value of integrating data from protein structures to analyse the evolution of key pathogens such as \textit{Betacoronavirus}. Finally, I demonstrated the feasibility of early infection detection through automated surveillance using popular markerless tracking methods in neuroscience research, paving the way for targeted interventions that mitigate mass culling and improve welfare outcomes.

These advances converge on a central imperative: pandemic preparedness demands systematic collaboration across the fields of agricultural sciences, veterinary medicine, microbiology, computational biology, and data science. This was exemplified by the third study, which combined videography, deep learning, veterinary science, and microbiology. This interdisciplinary imperative notwithstanding, pivoting across different scientific fields is known to carry a penalty on academic impact~\cite{hill2025}, which may appear as an obstacle for research despite the need for rapid interdisciplinary mobilisation in the context of pandemics.

From this thesis, three interconnected dimensions appear as priorities for strengthening preparedness frameworks: methodological innovation in data representation and analysis, ethical data governance structures that enable equitable knowledge distribution, and institutional mechanisms that reduce barriers to interdisciplinary research.
The convergence of technical, infrastructural, and structural reforms represents not only an academic ideal but also an operational necessity. In the wake of concerning trends in antimicrobial resistance~\cite{naghavi2025}, our ability to mobilise diverse scientific expertise swiftly and effectively will determine whether surveillance systems anticipate outbreaks or merely record their aftermath. Only through comprehensive approaches that integrate effective data handling, interdisciplinary collaboration, and institutional reform can we construct resilient systems capable of confronting the multifaceted challenges of pandemic preparedness.
