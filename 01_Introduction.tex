Human history has been profoundly shaped by waves of pathogen-borne epidemics. The perennial marks they have left on our species are wide-ranging, from our behaviour and social norms to our cellular and genetic material. A major factor underlying such epidemics is our interaction with other animal species, which has become increasingly frequent with migrations, the advent of domestication, and ecosystem-eroding technological advancements. Such interactions facilitate the occurrence of spillover events~\cite{vora2022, sikora2025}, where a pathogen prevalent in a host animal reservoir is transmitted to a nearby human population. Although progress in science, medicine, and public health awareness has greatly mitigated the frequency and impact of \emph{zoonotic} epidemics, the acceleration of globalisation and climate change in the 21st century is likely to change the balance~\cite{carlson2022}. Indeed, recent estimates posit a COVID-19-style pandemic being likely to occur once every 59 years on average~\cite{marani2021}. Among other factors, ecosystem modifications (e.g., deforestation, loss of biodiversity~\cite{ostfeld2009, keesing2010}) reduce buffers between zoonotic reservoirs and human populations, while disease-carrying vectors (e.g., mosquitoes, rodents) are found to expand their geographical ranges due to global warming~\cite{jones2008, ryan2019, johnson2020}. 

Since the early 2000s, non-governmental global organizations such as the \gls{who} and the World Bank have spearheaded efforts such as the \gls{goarn} and the \gls{gpmb} to strengthen international pandemic coordination~\cite{heymann2001, knobler2004, who2025}. Yet, as illustrated by the recent pandemics of SARS (2002-2004), swine flu (2009), and COVID-19 (2019-2023), preparedness against such events remains a Herculean task due to its complexity. Beyond logistical and sociopolitical issues, the scientific landscape of \emph{pandemic preparedness} is inherently interdisciplinary~\cite{fefferman2023}. For instance, microbiology is paramount to decipher the pathogen's structure and functions, ecology, and veterinary science to study the dynamics of animal reservoirs, statistics to model the progress of epidemics, information technology to develop software and accelerate computing tasks. Nowadays, \emph{data science} has become a backbone of pandemic preparedness, as it hinges on the analysis of heterogeneous datasets~\cite{leonelli2021}. These include wildlife monitoring footage, pathogenic genomes from wastewater, human mobility patterns, and electronic health records. Moreover, a substantial body of research on the COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the utility for large-scale, possibly population-wide, datasets such as better understand the dynamics of disease transmission, identify risk factors, and inform effective prevention and intervention strategies~\cite{grenfell2004, robishaw2021, vigfusson2021, attwood2022, matteson2023, khurana2024}. Thus, investment in data-driven research is crucial for our preparedness against the next pandemic.

This thesis aims to contribute to pandemic preparedness through data science advances, where a common underlying theme is \emph{vectorizing} pandemic-related data. Being able to represent complex data in a simple array of numbers is desirable for its efficient storage, scalability, and interoperability with various statistical frameworks. More specifically, I introduce \emph{phylo2vec}~\cite{penn2025}, a method for encoding phylogenetic trees (a representation of evolutionary relationships between entities) into a simple integer-based vector, enabling efficient data compression and facilitating their use in comparative analyses and potential machine learning applications. Second, I explore the potential of protein structures to better understand the evolution of pathogens. Using coronaviruses as an example, I used phylogenetic mixture modelling~\cite{pagel2005} to analyse the evolution of spike glycoproteins, which coronaviruses use to come in contact with a host cell and introduce viral material into it. Third, I propose a framework for video-based animal tracking~\cite{scheidwasser2025}, to enable early, non-invasive detection of infection in farm settings. Using broiler chickens as an example, I present a pilot study to detect \textit{E. coli} infection and a large video dataset for \textit{C. jejuni} infection. Together, these contributions highlight how efficient data representations can play a pivotal role in strengthening our capacity to anticipate, monitor, and respond to future pandemics.
